Geometry-Driven Benchmarking of Optimisers for Score Learning

A semester-long individual research project on how multimodal geometry, curvature and diffusion noise interact with optimisation when neural networks learn score functions.

High Distinction · 88/100 Individual Research PyTorch Diffusion Models Score Learning Optimisation Gaussian Mixture Models
SCDL3991 unit ↗ Supervisor: Dr Andi Han ↗ Request full report

Why study score learning this way?

Score-based diffusion models learn a vector field that points toward regions of higher probability under a noise-perturbed data distribution. Their generative performance therefore depends on learning this field accurately. In realistic datasets, however, the true score is unknown, making it difficult to separate optimisation error from the effects of multimodality, curvature and noise.

I used low-dimensional Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) as a controlled testbed. GMMs retain multimodal and curved score fields while giving access to the exact analytical score, so the learned network can be compared directly with ground truth.

∇x log pt(x) = 1 / (σ2 + t) [ Σk rk(x) μk − x ]

The analytical GMM score provides an exact supervision target instead of an estimated or surrogate target.

Three scientific questions

Q1

Symmetry vs asymmetry

How does the geometry of a mixture change optimiser performance when the underlying score is known exactly?

Q2

Why is K = 3 hard?

Why can the asymmetric three-component mixture be more difficult than mixtures with a larger number of modes?

Q3

Robustness to curvature

Which optimisers remain reliable when responsibility boundaries become irregular and curvature becomes anisotropic?

Experimental design

Controlled geometry

Four GMM configurations were studied. K = 2 and K = 4 served as relatively symmetric baselines; K = 3 was treated as the asymmetric, strongly anisotropic case; K = 5 acted as a verification experiment.

Exact score supervision

The analytical score was implemented directly in PyTorch, removing target-estimation error and allowing learned vector fields to be evaluated against ground truth.

Common neural architecture

A time-conditioned MLP used a two-layer time embedding with 16 SiLU units and a score network with two 128-unit SiLU hidden layers, outputting a two-dimensional score vector.

Systematic optimiser sweep

SGD, Momentum, SignedGD, Adam, Muon and Muon without momentum were compared over five learning rates and six epoch counts — 30 hyperparameter settings per optimiser/configuration, with multiple random seeds.

Noise as a geometry control

Lower t produces sharper mixture boundaries and higher curvature; larger t smooths the density. This allowed optimisation behaviour to be studied from sharper to smoother score fields.

Evaluation and reproducibility

An 80/20 train–validation split was used. RMSE and MAE measured magnitude error, while cosine similarity measured directional alignment. Fixed seeds and CSV logging supported reproducibility.

What the results reveal

1 · Mode count is not the whole story

Moving from symmetric K = 2 to symmetric K = 4 made learning somewhat harder but did not create a new failure mode. This suggests that simply adding modes is a weak proxy for optimisation difficulty.

2 · Geometry can dominate cardinality

K = 3 produced disproportionately high errors despite having fewer components than K = 4. The interpretation in the report is that irregular responsibility boundaries and anisotropic curvature force optimisers to handle steep and flat directions simultaneously.

3 · Adam was the geometry-robust baseline

Adam achieved the lowest mean RMSE for every mixture, while keeping cosine similarity very close to one. Its adaptive per-parameter scaling was the most robust to changing geometry in this benchmark.

4 · Momentum helps, but does not fully solve anisotropy

Muon and classical Momentum formed a strong second tier. Muon's mean RMSE rose from 0.040 at K = 2 to 0.164 at K = 3, showing considerably greater sensitivity than Adam.

5 · Sign-only updates have a geometric limit

SignedGD was surprisingly competitive in well-behaved symmetric settings, but degraded when curvature became more direction-dependent — consistent with a method that discards gradient magnitude information.

6 · SGD behaved as a negative control

Plain SGD was already weak at K = 2 and deteriorated further with complexity. Its behaviour highlighted how strongly fixed-step optimisation can depend on conditioning and learning-rate sensitivity.

Sample size and diffusion noise

More data substantially improved every optimiser in the difficult K = 3 setting. Adam's RMSE fell from 0.128 at n = 50 to 0.029 at n = 1000, while Muon fell from 0.263 to 0.083 and SGD from 0.562 to 0.156. Larger n therefore mitigated estimation difficulty, but did not erase the differences between optimisation methods.

Noise also interacted with geometry. In the K = 3 case, Adam's error peaked at the intermediate noise level t = 0.5 (RMSE 0.116), compared with 0.086 at t = 0.1 and 0.034 at t = 2.0. The report interprets this as a regime in which the score field is neither heavily smoothed nor effectively dominated by a single local mode, leaving strong direction-dependent curvature around asymmetric boundaries.

Research interpretation

Central lesson: optimisation difficulty was better explained by the geometry of the score field — especially curvature anisotropy and irregular mixture boundaries — than by the nominal number of mixture components. In this controlled setting, optimiser choice therefore depended on robustness to geometry, not simply on model or dataset size.

This result is useful because synthetic benchmarks can expose learning failures that would be expensive to isolate directly in large diffusion systems. The project does not claim that a two-dimensional GMM reproduces the full complexity of image or audio diffusion; rather, it provides an interpretable environment in which one mechanism can be studied precisely.

Limitations

  • The benchmark uses two-dimensional synthetic GMMs with only a small number of components.
  • A single relatively small MLP architecture was held fixed across experiments.
  • Learning rates and training durations came from a discrete static hyperparameter grid.
  • The optimiser set focuses on classical and diffusion-inspired first-order methods.
  • Evaluation targets score-learning accuracy rather than end-to-end sample generation quality.

These limitations are important: the findings are best read as a controlled foundation for studying optimisation mechanisms, not as a replacement for full-scale diffusion-model evaluation.

Where I would take this next

  • Richer asymmetric geometries: unequal variances, off-axis components and clustered-versus-isolated modes.
  • Higher dimensions and real data: test whether the observed hierarchy survives concentration effects and richer feature structure.
  • Broader optimiser families: include newer adaptive, quasi-Newton and explicitly curvature-aware methods.
  • Direct curvature measurement: estimate Hessian spectra or local curvature statistics and relate them quantitatively to optimiser error.

What this project changed for me

SCDL3991 was the point at which I became more interested in the mechanisms behind successful learning, not only the final benchmark score. Designing a controlled experiment around geometry taught me to ask which structural properties make optimisation robust, which apparent measures of complexity are misleading, and how carefully designed synthetic environments can expose behaviour that is difficult to diagnose at scale.

That perspective now motivates my broader interest in how the structure of data, objectives and learning environments shapes robust behaviour in larger AI systems.
