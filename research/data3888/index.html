Image21 — Deep Learning for Accessible Breast Cancer Histopathology Analysis

An interdisciplinary applied-AI project comparing CNN architectures for breast-cancer H&E patch classification and translating the results into an interpretable Shiny research prototype.

High Distinction · 88/100 Team / Technical Lead Deep Learning Transfer Learning Medical Imaging Grad-CAM Shiny
DATA3888 unit ↗ Research mentor: Manoj M. Wagle ↗ Request project report

Why this project?

Histopathology is central to identifying tumour morphology and immune-associated tissue patterns, but expert interpretation is time-intensive and difficult to scale. Image21 asked a deliberately narrower research question: how well can CNNs classify local H&E breast-cancer patches, and does increasing architectural complexity actually improve the result?

The aim was not to build an autonomous diagnostic system. The project instead treated histopathology as a challenging applied setting for comparing representation learning, transfer learning, class imbalance, model failure modes and transparent deployment.

Research questions

Q1

Can H&E morphology support useful classification?

Can CNNs distinguish early tumour, invasive tumour and immune-rich breast-cancer patches from standard H&E image data?

Q2

Does deeper automatically mean better?

How do scratch-trained CNNs compare with pretrained EfficientNet, ResNet and DenseNet architectures under the implemented project pipelines?

Q3

Can model behaviour be made inspectable?

Can class scores, cross-model comparison and Grad-CAM help expose agreement, disagreement and likely failure modes without presenting the model as a clinical authority?

Dataset and label design

The source data contained six biologically distinct labels derived from breast-cancer tumour microenvironment data. For the main three-class experiment, labels were collapsed before model training, so the deployed classifiers directly learned three broader superclasses. The six-class setting was retained separately for fine-grained biological analysis.

Data integrity and generalisation controls

Fixed data split

A single 70/15/15 train-validation-test split was created with seed 42 and saved permanently, so all models could use consistent data partitions.

Leakage check

Image identifiers were checked across the three folders, confirming zero exact filename overlap. The report explicitly notes that this does not by itself rule out patient-, slide- or staining-source leakage.

Balanced training

Training batches used balanced sampling so each superclass contributed approximately equally, while validation and test sets retained their natural distributions for realistic evaluation.

Augmentation

Training augmentation included horizontal and vertical flips and approximately ±25° rotation. Core TensorFlow/Keras experiments avoided colour jitter because H&E colour intensity may carry useful signal.

Regularisation

Depending on architecture, the pipelines used dropout, batch normalisation, label smoothing, early stopping, learning-rate reduction and model checkpointing.

Evaluation beyond accuracy

Macro-F1, per-class precision/recall/F1 and confusion matrices complemented accuracy so that performance on the majority class could not hide weaker minority-class behaviour.

Five architectures, one comparative question

Simple CNN

Lightweight three-block scratch-trained baseline used to establish a non-transfer-learning performance floor.

Deeper CNN

Five-stage scratch-trained CNN with batch normalisation and spatial dropout, testing whether depth alone improved feature learning.

EfficientNetB0

ImageNet-pretrained Keras backbone with a custom three-class head and two-phase transfer learning: frozen-backbone training followed by selective fine-tuning of upper layers.

ResNet18

ImageNet-pretrained residual network adapted to three classes, providing a mature residual-learning transfer baseline.

DenseNet121

Higher-capacity pretrained densely connected network used to test whether extensive feature reuse and added depth improved this small-patch histopathology task.

Shared comparison goal

The project compared implemented pipelines rather than claiming a perfectly controlled architecture-only ablation: preprocessing, frameworks, augmentation and training schedules differed between some models.

Central model-comparison lesson: larger or deeper was not automatically better. ResNet18 produced the strongest numerical result, EfficientNetB0 provided the best deployment trade-off, and DenseNet121 demonstrated that greater capacity alone did not guarantee stronger generalisation under the available data and tuning budget.

The report deliberately treats this as a pipeline-level finding, not proof that residual connections alone caused ResNet18's advantage. The models differed in architecture, framework, preprocessing, augmentation and training configuration, so the most defensible conclusion is that data fit, transfer-learning strategy, optimisation and architecture design jointly mattered more than nominal depth.

Where did the strongest models still fail?

Aggregate accuracy concealed a consistent class-level weakness. In the row-normalised confusion matrices, Immune recall was 79.1% for ResNet18 and 76.4% for EfficientNetB0, below their recall for Early Tumour and Invasive Tumour. The same broad pattern appeared across architectures.

The project identified several plausible reasons: the Immune superclass combined biologically heterogeneous CD8+ T cells and macrophage subtypes; immune cells can appear within or near tumour regions; the 100 × 100 patch size limits tissue context; and the class had smaller support than Invasive Tumour. These explanations remain hypotheses rather than independently validated causal mechanisms.

Best classifier vs best deployed model

ResNet18 — strongest numerical model

88.65% test accuracy and 0.8796 macro-F1. Residual connections and pretrained feature extraction may have helped, but the project does not claim a clean causal architecture ablation.

EfficientNetB0 — strongest deployment trade-off

87.57% accuracy and 0.8686 macro-F1, with reliable integration into the TensorFlow/Keras Shiny workflow, live inference and Grad-CAM visualisation.

This distinction became one of the most important engineering lessons of Image21: the numerically best model is not automatically the most useful deployed model. Deployment also requires stable integration, acceptable inference cost, interpretability support and a clear understanding of failure modes.

Image21: from model benchmark to inspectable system

100 × 100 H&E patch CNN inference Three-class output Class-score display Grad-CAM inspection

The Shiny application exposed two interaction modes. In upload mode, a user could supply a new patch for live inference. In reference-case mode, labelled internal examples could be inspected to understand how the model behaved on known cases. Reference examples were demonstration cases, not external validation.

Interpretability without overclaiming

Grad-CAM was used to show which image regions most influenced a selected class score. This can help check whether the network appears to focus on tissue rather than blank background or obvious artefacts, but it does not prove that the learned decision rule is biologically correct.

A particularly useful presentation example showed that all five models could predict the same patch correctly while assigning very different output scores. That observation reinforced why a single correct prediction or a visually persuasive heat map is insufficient evidence for clinical trust.

Reproducibility

The submitted reproducibility package included the final report, LaTeX source, figures, code, Shiny application, saved outputs, prediction exports and environment files. The report also proposed a traceable prediction-export schema including image ID, ground-truth label, model, predicted label, class scores, checkpoint identity, training-pipeline provenance and audit fields for test membership.

Why this matters Reproducibility was treated as part of the modelling result rather than an afterthought: model comparisons only become meaningful when evaluation cases, checkpoints and pipeline provenance can be traced consistently.

Responsible-AI boundary

Image21 was explicitly designed as a research and education prototype, not a clinical diagnostic device. Grad-CAM is a coarse post-hoc attention method, not a mechanistic biological explanation. Softmax outputs were not calibrated and should not be interpreted as medical probabilities.

The project therefore used interpretability to support inspection rather than to create a false sense of certainty. Human review remains essential, especially for ambiguous tumour–immune boundaries and out-of-distribution data.

Limitations

  • Limited spatial context: 100 × 100 patches may omit tissue architecture visible only at larger scales.
  • Same-source evaluation: the models were not externally validated on an independent hospital dataset.
  • Patient/slide leakage risk: exact image overlap was checked, but higher-level provenance required further auditing.
  • Stain variation: laboratory, scanner and preparation differences could reduce external generalisability.
  • Class heterogeneity: the Immune superclass combined visually different immune-cell populations.
  • Pipeline heterogeneity: model frameworks and training schedules differed, limiting strict causal architecture comparisons.
  • Interpretability limits: Grad-CAM cannot establish biological correctness or clinical validity.

Where I would take this next

  • Patient-level splitting: explicitly prevent patient or slide provenance from crossing dataset partitions.
  • External validation: evaluate on independent hospital or laboratory data.
  • Stain normalisation: test robustness to preparation and scanner variation.
  • Probability calibration: distinguish confidence scores from calibrated predictive uncertainty.
  • Broader tissue context: move from local patches toward larger fields of view or whole-slide methods.
  • Expert evaluation: involve pathologists in structured review of model errors and Grad-CAM outputs.
  • New architectures cautiously: evaluate transformer or CNN–transformer systems only with sufficient data and comparable validation.

What this project changed for me

DATA3888 gave me a different view of AI research from my individual optimisation work. In a real applied pipeline, performance depended not only on model architecture but also on data design, transfer learning, class balance, evaluation choices, interpretability, deployment constraints and the discipline to state what the system could not claim.

The project reinforced a theme that has become central to my research interests: greater nominal complexity does not automatically produce better or more trustworthy intelligence. What matters is how representation, learning strategy, evaluation and environment interact to produce behaviour that is robust, interpretable and genuinely useful.
