Image21 — Deep Learning for Accessible Breast Cancer Histopathology Analysis

A team-based applied AI project comparing CNN architectures for H&E histopathology classification and translating the results into an interpretable Shiny research prototype.

High Distinction · 88/100 Team / Technical Lead Deep Learning Transfer Learning Grad-CAM Shiny
DATA3888 unit ↗ Manoj M. Wagle ↗ Request project report

Project question

We investigated how accurately deep-learning models could classify biologically distinct breast-cancer cell and tissue patterns from H&E image patches, and whether increasing neural-network complexity actually improved performance.

What we built

The team compared five architectures — Simple CNN, Deeper CNN, EfficientNetB0, ResNet18 and DenseNet121 — under a shared evaluation framework. The project also produced an interactive Shiny prototype that combined model predictions with Grad-CAM visualisations to expose influential image regions and make agreement, disagreement and failure modes easier to inspect.

My role

I served as a team and technical lead, contributing to project coordination, Shiny application development, EfficientNetB0 deployment interpretation, model-comparison framing, presentation structure and final technical integration. I helped connect the modelling results with the live application, Grad-CAM interpretation and the project's clinical-safety limitations.

Main finding

ResNet18 achieved the strongest reported three-class result, while EfficientNetB0 was close behind and was selected as the deployed model because of its balance of predictive performance, practical inference and reliable Grad-CAM integration. DenseNet121 underperformed despite greater capacity, reinforcing the conclusion that model size alone does not determine generalisation.

Engineering lesson: the numerically strongest model is not automatically the best deployment choice. Accuracy, interpretability, integration, data fit and failure analysis all matter.

Research mentor

Manoj M. Wagle ↗

Manoj M. Wagle is a University of Sydney researcher with MIT CSAIL research affiliation whose work spans interpretable deep learning, biomedical AI, neuroscience and computational biology. His work helped provide an interdisciplinary research context for a project that sat between machine learning and biomedical data.

Responsible AI boundary

Image21 was designed as a research and education prototype, not as a clinical diagnostic device. Grad-CAM was used as a qualitative interpretability aid rather than proof of biological mechanism, and model softmax outputs were not presented as calibrated clinical probabilities.

What this project changed for me

Image21 showed me that successful AI research requires more than a strong headline metric. It requires careful choices about data, evaluation, interpretability, deployment and the limitations of what a model is actually entitled to claim.
