THE STARTING POINT

From “which optimiser wins?” to “what makes optimisation hard?”

My SCDL3991 Dalyell research project examined score learning on Gaussian mixture models under diffusion noise. Rather than treating optimiser comparison as a leaderboard, I tried to make the problem diagnostic: vary the mixture geometry, sample size, noise level and optimiser while holding the rest of the experimental system under control.

That shift in framing mattered. It turned a broad empirical question into something closer to a scientific instrument: if performance changes, which part of the problem changed with it?

02

THE TECHNICAL WORK

A reproducible benchmark, not a one-off notebook.

I built a PyTorch experimental pipeline comparing six optimisation methods across multiple Gaussian-mixture configurations, diffusion-noise levels and sample sizes. The final study comprised more than 10,000 runs, with consistent validation metrics and controlled comparisons designed to separate optimiser behaviour from data geometry.

THE RESEARCH SIGNAL

Geometry changed the story.

The strongest lesson was that difficulty was not explained by mode count alone. Asymmetry and curvature anisotropy could destabilise non-adaptive methods much more strongly, while Adam remained comparatively stable across the benchmark. The result pushed me to think about optimisation through the geometry of the learning problem, not just the optimiser name.

The useful outcome was not simply “Adam performed best”; it was learning how to design experiments that reveal why optimisation behaviour changes.
