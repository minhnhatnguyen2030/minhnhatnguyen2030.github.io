2030: If Disease Y Arrives

A question written before the PhD and sent four years forward: what kind of AI would I actually trust beside humans when evidence is incomplete, the world is changing, and a wrong answer can become a real action?

Written in Sydney · Saturday, 22 August 2026 · To be reopened in 2030

2030

A future I cannot see

This is not a prediction. It is a stress test.

Suppose an unfamiliar infectious disease appears across several cities. Reports conflict, models drift, evidence arrives late, and synthetic media makes the information environment harder to trust. Call it Disease Y. The name is fictional. The research problem is not.

Scientific note. “Disease Y” is only a narrative placeholder for a future high-uncertainty crisis. It is not a prediction of a pandemic in 2030 and not a proposal to design biological agents. The research direction is reliable, verification-grounded multimodal AI under uncertainty.

01

The question I carried out of 2021

During the Delta outbreak in Ho Chi Minh City, I did not encounter a pandemic as a neat machine-learning dataset. Laboratories measured one part of reality. Epidemiologists inferred another. Models tried to see around the corner. Communicators translated changing evidence. Volunteers and healthcare workers turned decisions into action. Then new observations arrived, and yesterday’s assumptions could fail.

What stayed with me was not one forecast or one technology. It was the loop: no person possessed the whole answer, and useful intelligence came from repeatedly confronting belief with new evidence.

A crisis is not solved by one prediction. It is solved by a loop that keeps meeting reality.

evidence→ belief→ prediction→ action→ new evidence→ revision

2026 · Working PhD direction

Reliable Multimodal Scientific Agents Under Uncertainty

How can an AI agent maintain calibrated beliefs when evidence is incomplete, conflicting or out-of-distribution; determine what evidence or feedback it needs next; and convert hypotheses into executable, independently verifiable computational experiments rather than unsupported language-model conclusions?

Problem 1 Reason reliably Track evidence, provenance, contradiction, uncertainty and distribution shift — and abstain when evidence is insufficient.
Problem 2 Learn efficiently Select useful data, memory and feedback rather than repeatedly retraining or accumulating context without discrimination.
Problem 3 Verify experimentally Turn hypotheses into reproducible computational tests whose outputs can be independently checked.

Disease Y is the stress test, not the thesis. The AI contribution has to survive outside epidemiology — in machine learning, optimisation, simulation, statistics and other computational domains — before it deserves trust in a high-stakes crisis.

02

SIM should not be an oracle

I call the thought experiment SIM: the Scientific Intelligence Machine. The name sounds grand; the useful version is deliberately less mystical. SIM is a scientific teammate that can preserve where evidence came from, maintain competing hypotheses, decide what information is missing, run bounded computational experiments and expose the result to independent verification.

Its most important sentence may be the least impressive: I do not know yet. Uncertainty is not something to hide behind fluent language. It is information about what should be investigated next.

Objective determines direction; uncertainty determines the next experiment.

01

Perception

Read text, images, tables, time series and maps without pretending the modalities are interchangeable.

02

Evidence memory

Preserve source, time, confidence and provenance instead of collapsing observations into opaque context.

03

Uncertainty

Maintain competing explanations and calibrated reasons for confidence or doubt.

04

Planner

Choose whether to answer, retrieve more evidence, ask a human, compute, simulate or abstain.

05

Sandbox

Run code and simulations inside controlled, permissioned and reversible environments.

06

Verifier

Use tests, statistics, simulators or domain evaluators to check what generation alone cannot guarantee.

03

Why 2026 changed the question

In 2021 there was no generally capable multimodal agent beside the team. By 2026, models can read, see, write code and increasingly use tools. That makes reliability more consequential: a fluent error can now become an action.

Jeff Dean’s February 2026 Princeton lecture gives this page a useful external reference point. He discusses a future in which humans may coordinate many AI agents and, in the Q&A, addresses both synthetic misinformation and techniques that evaluate multiple candidate reasoning trajectories rather than trusting one answer. For this research direction, the lesson is simple: generation needs evidence, uncertainty and verification around it.

04

Now turn the machine on

Imagine that the calendar really does say 2030. Disease Y appears. SIM receives clinical reports, papers, epidemiological time series, maps, laboratory summaries, official announcements, social-media claims, simulator outputs and expert assessments. Some sources are trustworthy. Some are stale. Some conflict because the world has changed. Some may have been manipulated.

Its job is not to compress everything into one confident paragraph. Its job is to keep the chain of reasoning inspectable while reality changes underneath it.

08:10 · An anomaly appears.

Hospital and wastewater signals rise while reported case counts remain flat. SIM keeps several hypotheses alive.

09:40 · Evidence conflicts.

A widely shared video contradicts surveillance data. SIM checks provenance, timing and whether apparently independent sources are copying one another.

11:20 · Uncertainty becomes a question.

Instead of guessing, SIM identifies the next observation that would best distinguish the leading explanations.

14:00 · An experiment runs.

Competing models are tested in a sandbox. Code, assumptions, measurements and failures are logged; a separate evaluator checks the result.

16:30 · A human decides.

SIM presents evidence, uncertainty and options. Reasoning permission never becomes unrestricted execution permission.

05

What part of 2021 Thomas should a machine replace?

Not the physician. Not the laboratory scientist. Not the epidemiologist. Not the policymaker. Not the person accountable for another human life. What I would like to replace is the fragile information bottleneck: the hours spent searching, comparing, copying, remembering, recomputing and wondering whether something important has been missed.

A machine may read more than I can read and run more experiments than I can run. That does not give it judgment by default. It earns a place on the team only if it can show where its conclusions came from, expose uncertainty, accept correction and stay within boundaries humans can understand.

SIM should be able to

  • retrieve and compare evidence;
  • quantify uncertainty and contradiction;
  • suggest informative next observations;
  • run sandboxed computational experiments;
  • record provenance, failures and revisions;
  • abstain and escalate to a human.

SIM should not be able to

  • hide uncertainty behind fluent language;
  • treat self-agreement as independent verification;
  • turn a recommendation into unrestricted action;
  • bypass privacy, credentials or permissions;
  • autonomously perform high-risk wet-lab biological design;
  • claim authority that belongs to accountable humans.

Every point in an epidemic curve is somebody. The goal is not an AI that replaces scientists during the next crisis, but one that helps them confront uncertainty faster without hiding it.

06

Questions for the person who opens this in 2030

I do not know whether I will begin the Integrated M.S.–Ph.D. path at KAIST, whether this exact proposal will survive graduate school, or whether the field will move so quickly that SIM will sound quaint. That uncertainty is part of why this page exists.

  • Did I make it to KAIST?
  • Did reliable scientific agents become the research problem I actually pursued?
  • Did uncertainty become something agents could use to choose what to investigate next?
  • Did verification become normal, or did fluent self-evaluation remain enough?
  • Does SIM exist in any meaningful form?
  • If a real crisis arrived, were we more prepared than we were in 2021?
