2030: If Disease Y Arrives

A research time capsule written before the PhD: one question sent forward from Saigon 2021 and Sydney 2026 — what kind of AI would I actually trust beside humans when the world becomes uncertain again?

Written in Sydney · Saturday, 22 August 2026 · To be reopened in 2030

2030

A message to a future I cannot see

This is not a prediction. It is a stress test.

Suppose an unfamiliar infectious disease appears across several cities. Evidence is incomplete, models disagree, the information environment is noisy, and some media may be synthetic. Call it Disease Y. The name is fictional. The research question is real.

Scientific note. “Disease Y” is used here only as a narrative placeholder for a future high-uncertainty crisis. It is not a prediction of a pandemic in 2030 and not a proposal to design biological agents. The underlying research direction is reliable, verification-grounded multimodal AI under uncertainty.

2021 · Saigon A human network tried to turn tests, reports, models and changing evidence into action during the Delta outbreak.
→
2026 · Sydney I ask whether some of that cognitive burden can become a rigorous AI research problem: uncertainty, adaptation and verification.
→
2030 · ? The test is not whether a machine sounds intelligent. It is whether humans can trust its evidence, experiments and limits.

01

Before there was an agent, there was a team

In 2021, I encountered a pandemic not as a clean dataset but as a moving system. A laboratory measured one part of reality. Epidemiologists interpreted another. Models tried to see around the corner. Public communication translated changing evidence into language people could act on. Volunteers and healthcare workers carried decisions into the physical world. Then new observations arrived, and yesterday’s assumptions could fail.

I was young enough to keep asking naive questions: Why test so many people? Where does the data come from? What if the forecast is wrong? How do we know when a model should change its mind? Around me were people working in molecular testing, public health, communication, artificial intelligence, biotechnology and scientific advice. None of them possessed the whole answer.

A crisis is not solved by one prediction. It is solved by a loop that keeps meeting reality.

evidence→belief→prediction→action→new evidence→revision

02

BioMAFIA: Team 1.0

Near the end of that summer, a student biotechnology competition needed a team name. My father glanced at a copy of The Bomber Mafia and suggested, with unnecessary satisfaction, BioMAFIA. The joke stayed. So did the feeling behind it: difficult biological problems need different kinds of minds around the same table.

BioMAFIA →

The 2021 chapter is where the name began. Professor Truyen Tran also appears in that story; his 2020 talk AI giữa đại dịch Covid-19 asked what AI could realistically contribute during the pandemic.

From 2030, I want to imagine a second version of that team. Not a machine replacing doctors, scientists, epidemiologists or decision-makers. A machine replacing some of the information bottleneck that a human like me could only handle manually in 2021: searching, comparing, remembering, checking, recomputing and asking what evidence is still missing.

2026 · Working PhD direction

Reliable Multimodal Scientific Agents Under Uncertainty

How can an AI agent maintain calibrated beliefs when evidence is incomplete, conflicting or out-of-distribution; actively determine what evidence or feedback it needs next; and convert hypotheses into executable, independently verifiable computational experiments rather than unsupported language-model conclusions?

PhD problem 1 Reason reliably Track evidence, provenance, contradictions, uncertainty and distribution shift — and abstain when the evidence is not enough.
PhD problem 2 Learn efficiently Choose useful data, feedback and experiences rather than repeatedly retraining or filling context indiscriminately.
PhD problem 3 Run verified experiments Turn hypotheses into reproducible computational tests with independent evaluators, logs and measurable failure.

Disease Y is the stress test, not the thesis. The scientific contribution must remain general enough to evaluate in machine learning, optimisation, simulation, statistics and other computational domains before it is trusted in a high-stakes crisis setting.

03

SIM: the Scientific Intelligence Machine

I call the thought experiment SIM — the Scientific Intelligence Machine. “Superintelligence machine” is the deliberately provocative nickname; the research version is much less mystical. SIM is an agent that can observe heterogeneous evidence, preserve where it came from, represent competing hypotheses, choose what to investigate next, execute bounded computational experiments and ask an independent process to verify the result.

Its most important capability may be the sentence current systems are often reluctant to say: I do not know yet. Uncertainty is not a defect to hide. It is information about what the system should investigate next.

Objective determines direction; uncertainty determines the next experiment.

01

Multimodal perception

Read text, images, tables, time series, maps and domain-specific representations without pretending they are interchangeable.

02

Evidence & provenance memory

Store observations with source, time, confidence and lineage instead of collapsing everything into one opaque context.

03

Belief & uncertainty

Maintain competing explanations, calibrated confidence and explicit reasons for doubt.

04

Planner

Choose whether to answer, retrieve, ask a human, simulate, run a computation or abstain.

05

Sandboxed executor

Run code and simulations inside controlled environments with permissions, budgets and reversible actions.

06

Independent verifier

Score outputs with deterministic tests, statistical tests, simulators or domain-specific evaluators whenever possible.

04

Why 2026 changed the question

In 2021 there was no generally capable multimodal foundation-model agent beside our team. By 2026, models can read, see, write code, call tools, maintain long contexts and increasingly operate as agents. That is exactly why reliability matters more, not less. A fluent error can now become an action.

Jeff Dean’s Princeton lecture in February 2026 captures both sides of the opportunity. He describes a future in which one human may coordinate many AI agents; later, when asked about near-term fears, he points to realistic synthetic misinformation; and when asked about hallucination, he discusses generating multiple candidate rollouts and evaluating them rather than trusting a single answer. For my research question, those ideas meet at one point: generation needs evidence, uncertainty and verification around it.

05

Turn the machine on

Now imagine that the calendar really does say 2030. Disease Y appears. SIM receives clinical reports, papers, epidemiological time series, maps, mobility patterns, wastewater signals, diagnostic images, laboratory summaries, official announcements, social-media claims, simulator outputs and expert assessments. Some are trustworthy. Some are stale. Some disagree because the world has changed. Some may be manipulated.

SIM’s job is not to compress all of that into one confident paragraph. Its job is to keep the chain of reasoning auditable while the situation changes underneath it.

08:10 · An anomaly appears.

Hospital and wastewater signals rise, but official case counts remain flat. SIM keeps several hypotheses alive rather than deciding prematurely.

09:40 · The evidence conflicts.

A widely shared video contradicts the surveillance record. SIM checks provenance, temporal consistency and whether apparently independent sources are actually copying one another.

11:20 · Uncertainty becomes a query.

Instead of guessing, SIM identifies the next observation that would best distinguish the leading hypotheses and reports why that observation matters.

14:00 · A computational experiment runs.

Competing models are fitted in a sandbox. Code, assumptions, seeds, measurements and failures are logged. An independent evaluator checks the output.

16:30 · A human decides.

SIM presents evidence, uncertainty and options. It does not convert reasoning permission into unrestricted execution permission. Consequential action remains governed by humans and institutions.

06

Replace Thomas?

The provocative version of the idea is simple: could SIM replace the role Thomas played in 2021?

Not the physician. Not the laboratory scientist. Not the epidemiologist. Not the policymaker. Not the person responsible for another human life. What I would like to replace is the fragile human bottleneck between fragments of information: the hours spent searching, comparing, copying, remembering, recomputing and wondering whether something important has been missed.

A machine can read more than I could read, remember more than I could remember and run more experiments than I could run. But scale does not automatically produce judgment. The machine earns a place on the team only if it can show where its conclusions came from, expose its uncertainty, accept correction and remain inside boundaries humans can understand.

SIM should be allowed to

  • retrieve and compare evidence;
  • quantify uncertainty and detect contradictions;
  • suggest informative next observations;
  • run sandboxed computational experiments;
  • record provenance, code, failures and revisions;
  • abstain and escalate to a human.

SIM should not be allowed to

  • hide uncertainty behind fluent language;
  • treat self-agreement as independent verification;
  • turn a recommendation into unrestricted external action;
  • bypass privacy, credentials or operational permissions;
  • autonomously perform high-risk wet-lab biological design;
  • claim authority that belongs to accountable humans.

07

One life is still not a statistic

The reason to build a system like this is not that machines are more interesting than people. It is the opposite. Machine learning naturally pulls attention toward scale: more samples, more parameters, more tokens, more agents, more compute. The lesson I carried out of 2021 was that every point in an epidemic curve is somebody.

So the measure of SIM cannot be how persuasive its demo looks. It has to be whether it helps people reach better, faster and more defensible decisions without pretending to know what it does not know.

The goal is not an AI that replaces scientists during the next crisis. It is an AI that helps scientists confront uncertainty faster without hiding it from them.

08

Questions for the person who opens this in 2030

I do not know whether I will begin the Integrated M.S.–Ph.D. path at KAIST, whether this exact proposal will survive contact with graduate school, or whether the field will move so quickly that SIM will sound quaint four years from now. That uncertainty is part of the point of leaving this page here.

  • Did I make it to KAIST?
  • Did reliable multimodal agents become the research problem I actually pursued?
  • Did uncertainty become something agents could use to choose their next experiment?
  • Did independent verification become normal, or did fluent self-evaluation remain enough?
  • Did humans learn how to coordinate teams of agents without surrendering responsibility to them?
  • Does SIM exist in any meaningful sense?
  • Was there a Disease Y?
  • And if there was: were we more prepared than we were in 2021?
